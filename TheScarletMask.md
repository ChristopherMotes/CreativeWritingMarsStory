## Secondshima

From the seventy-second floor of the grandest tower in Secondshima, Stevie Tanaka could see the dome as a machine that had decided it liked freedom more than order.

It was the largest dome on Mars, and it looked like a city built from optimism and stubbornness. The streets below glittered with steel and glass, but nothing was neat. Not the blocks, not the traffic, not the politics. Every avenue bent toward a different idea of liberty. There were markets where women in heavy coats haggled under the light of ad-drones, and outside the apartment blocks men in work shirts checked the condition of their sidearms the way other men checked the weather. In Secondshima, the right to keep and bear arms was not an argument; it was a condition of life. Every house had its own rough architecture of self-defense, and every citizen, each male from sixteen to sixty-five, lived with the knowledge that the state was not the last word on violence.

Below, the city rippled with the kind of motion that made a man think law was less a shield than a negotiation. Police were present, but not everywhere. Private security was common, especially among the wealthy. Drones skimmed the avenues ahead of the powerful like obedient crows. The elite moved behind layered barriers, their routes chosen, their lives protected by a thousand small systems. The lower and middle classes did not disappear from his sight—they looked down on the protected upper circles from below, with a contempt that was equal parts pride and suspicion. To them, the elite's private security was not safety but a sign of weakness: men who had become so afraid of the world that they had to buy a wall around it. They thought of themselves as the heirs of ordinary people who kept the machinery of civilization running with their own hands—workers, mechanics, sailors, and contractors who stood up before anyone else and took responsibility for themselves. Liberty, to them, was not something granted by a polished and protected class; it was a habit of standing your ground, carrying your own weight, and refusing to ask permission to live. The middle classes especially saw private security as another form of policy, a system of controls that cramped their liberty and told them they were not trusted to handle their own streets, their own homes, or their own risks. They preferred the rough edge of self-reliance to the clean illusion of protection.

From his window, Stevie's mind's eye could see a woman at a street corner stop to adjust the holster on her hip before stepping onto a crowded tram. He saw a teenager in a school jacket carrying a rifle case with the casualness of a commuter carrying a briefcase. He saw the old men in the plazas, unhurried, their hands never far from the grips at their belts. In Secondshima, the citizen was not meant to be helpless. He was meant to be capable.

The city looked free because it had chosen to build freedom into its bones. The law kept to a brief Bill of Rights, the courts were crowded and impatient, and no one wanted to be imprisoned without a clear case reaching a jury. There was no illusion that government was benevolent. The people had built the right to resist into the architecture of the place itself. And from that height, with the dome glowing amber around him and the sky a pale Mars red, the man could almost believe that liberty was not a slogan here, but a city plan.

Unbeknownst to the tourists and other viewers around him, Stevie Tanaka was the team leader for the Scarlet Mask's heaviest-hitting team. He'd been that way for six months. The Scarlet Mask and his teams were vigilantes, even in Secondshima. They were wanted for murder, robbery, aggravated assault, and every other violent crime. They identified themselves as filling a gap in the system—a gap the police were supposed to fill: stopping organized crime and invasions by criminals from other domes. The police failed. In Stevie's view, it was both corruption and the constraints of Secondshima's Bill of Rights. Someone needed to operate outside of corruption and the law to fill that gap. Stevie was here clearing his head for a major operation. The Vozhdnya Cartel from Vostoshima had pushed into Secondshima. Stevie and his team were ready to make them pay. In less than two Martian days, Stevie's life would be over.
