# The Government Reaction

## Part One: The Police

Officer Keisha Washington was one of the first on scene when the overdose clusters started. She'd been a beat cop for twelve years, back when Vostoshima was smaller and you knew your territory by the sounds it made at night. She still knows those sounds. Now she hears them amplified by panic.

Marcus Reeves sees her on the welding level one morning, talking to a group of teenagers near the fabrication sector. She's not threatening them. She's asking questions. She's listening. When one of the kids mentions a friend who's hospitalized, Washington's face registers actual concern. Not the performative kind. The real thing.

"That's Keisha," one of the other welders tells Marcus. "Good cop. Got three kids herself. When her youngest got hooked on something three years ago, she fought to get him into treatment instead of arrest. Used her own time."

Washington is also spearheading the expanded DARE programs and youth athletic leagues. Not the box-checking kind of policing. Real engagement. She knows most of the teenagers in her precinct by name.

But during the raids responding to the overdose crisis, Officer Patterson gets carried away. He breaks bones on suspects that turned out to be innocent. The review board calls it excessive but justified given circumstances. Patterson remains on duty.

Marcus hears about this through the community boards. A neighbor posts about knowing someone who was roughed up during a raid. The story doesn't make the news the way Washington's heroism does.

Two weeks after Patricia Chen is hospitalized, a raid on a Free Mars New Generation distribution point goes wrong. The details are confused at first. Marcus hears fragments at the medical facility, in hab-unit message boards, in the break room conversations of other workers.

Washington was providing overwatch for a unit moving on a suspected stash house. A civilian was in the building—not involved, just in the wrong place. When the raid went kinetic, the civilian panicked and ran directly into the line of fire. Washington saw it happening. She moved to pull them to safety.

She took the fatal shot doing it.

The community boards explode. There are flowers at the police precinct. There are people calling for war against the crime families. There are people questioning why raids are happening at all.

And there's a separate thread in the community boards about Patterson's excessive force. About how many cops are genuinely trying to help and how many are using the crisis to consolidate power and squeeze harder.

Dr. Chen mentions it quietly to Marcus during Sophie's next check-up. "I treated her for a minor injury three months ago," Chen says. "She was kind. She was trying so hard to help."

Marcus thinks about Washington talking to teenagers on the welding level. He thinks about a woman with three kids who chose to get her son treatment instead of arrest. He thinks about her pulling a stranger to safety and receiving a bullet for it.

He also thinks about Patterson, still on duty.

He thinks about what happens next.

## Part Two: The Politicians

Councilman Torres holds a press conference the day after Washington's death is confirmed. The cameras are there immediately. He stands in front of her photo and speaks with genuine urgency: "We have a drug crisis in our dome. We need stronger enforcement, better coordination with police, expanded criminal penalties."

Marcus watches this at a hab-unit community board. Torres means every word.

Councilwoman Liu moves quickly: "The root cause is inadequate government oversight. We need to strengthen our capacity to monitor and control criminal activity."

Dr. Patel arrives at it differently: "Drug addiction isn't a criminal problem, it's a health problem. We should be treating users, not punishing them."

His heart is genuine.

Stephanie Wong approaches it carefully. She's spent years watching how Vostoshima functions. Something about it seems different from comparable domes. More stable. More orderly. Crime exists, but it doesn't spiral. Territorial disputes happen but don't metastasize.

"Community police programs are effective," Wong says. "Long-term treatment infrastructure prevents addiction. We should build on what's proving successful. Something about how this dome operates is working better than the historical comparisons."

Councilor Helena Vasquez has noticed the same thing. She draws the opposite conclusion: "This dome operates on an implicit bargain. We tolerate criminal activity in exchange for orderliness. That's corruption, and we need to break it. We need legitimate government control over all aspects of our society, not this... arrangement we've settled into."

The debate unfolds between them. Torres and Liu want to enforce harder. Patel wants to treat and prevent. Wong wants to understand what's working and build on it. Vasquez wants to tear it all down and rebuild.

Marcus reads their proposals without fully understanding why something about them unsettles him. Wong and Vasquez are both observing the same stability. They're just drawing opposite conclusions about what it means.

He thinks about Officer Washington, who died trying to police a system she could see.

He thinks about politicians trying to solve problems in a dome they're not quite seeing clearly.
